Your video chat

🎬 5 quick questions ⏱️ 30–60 sec each 💬 Just a friendly chat
Hi there

Thank you!

You're about to record a short video chat to help other young people who get caught up in stuck thinking, like worries or memories that make us feel bad. Your story and your tips could really help someone your age feel less alone. It's really kind of you to help – thank you!

Have a read through this with a parent or carer before your chat.

The plan

What actually happens

  • You'll have a video call with Nikki, a university student who is working on a project about stuck thinking. It's just a friendly chat.
  • Nikki will ask you 5 questions. You answer each one in your own words, taking about 30–60 seconds for each answer.
  • The call gets recorded and cut into a few short clips.
  • There are no right or wrong answers. It's all about your experience.
  • If you fumble or want to start again, no worries at all. We can redo any answer.
  • Nikki might ask you to try an answer again so that we can get some different takes and then pick the best one.
  • We use a made-up name for you, so no one watching knows it's you.
  • You (and your parent or carer) get to watch the clips and say yes before any are used. Nothing goes in without your ok.
Camera ready

Set up your phone

  • Join the call on your phone using the Zoom app. Download it and log in before the session (a parent can help).
  • Prop your phone at eye level and far enough away from you that we can see your face and upper body.
  • During the chat, look at the screen or the edge of your phone, not straight into the camera. (Looking right into the camera can feel a bit intense on video.)
  • Pop your phone somewhere it won't wobble (e.g. you can prop it up against your laptop screen).
Filming spot

Light and background

✓ Do

  • Sit facing or next to a window or light, so the light shines on your face
  • Pick a plain, calm spot: a blank wall, a wall with a few nice pictures, or the garden

✕ Don't

  • Sit with a bright window or light behind you (it turns you into a dark shadow)
  • Film somewhere that looks like a classroom or office
Dress for camera

What to wear

✓ Great choices

  • Comfy, everyday clothes you feel good in
  • Plain, solid colours – soft calm ones look best (blue, green, beige, pastels)
  • Hair neat and out of your face if you can
  • Cultural or religious clothing, if you usually wear it

✕ Best to skip

  • Busy patterns, thin stripes or tiny prints (they don't look good on camera)
  • Big logos or slogans
  • Anything itchy or annoying to wear
The important bit

How to give a great answer

  • Talk like you're chatting to a friend who is between 10–14 years old. Keep it real.
  • Keep each answer to about 30–60 seconds. Short and natural is great.
  • Start your answer using words from the question, so it makes sense on its own.
    Question: "What helps you break free from stuck thinking?"
    Good start: "What helps me break free from stuck thinking is…"
    Confusing start: "What helps me is…" ← a viewer wouldn't know what you mean.
  • Jot down a few dot points to remind you. Keep them near your camera so you can glance up and still look at the screen.
  • Practise a couple of times, but don't memorise it. Reading a script word-for-word sounds a bit robotic. We want the real you.
  • "Um" and "like" are totally fine! They make you sound natural.
  • Use simple, everyday words.
  • When you've finished, say "that's all for that one." It helps Nikki know you're done. (We cut this out of the final clip.)
Have a think

The 5 questions

Tap each question to see a way or two to start, plus a short example. There's no need to write full answers – just come with a few ideas from your own experience.

What helps you notice that you're caught up in stuck thinking?

Ways to start
  • "When I start getting stuck in my thoughts, I notice…"
  • "I can usually catch my stuck thinking if I pay attention to…"
Example"When I start getting stuck in my thoughts, I notice I keep replaying the same bad memory over and over, like a video on repeat. My chest also feels kind of tight."

What usually helps you break free from stuck thinking?

Ways to start
  • "What usually helps me break free from stuck thinking is…"
  • "When I notice I'm caught up in stuck thinking, I try to…"

The important part: tell us why it works – the best activities really grab your full attention, so there's no room left for the sticky thoughts.

Example"What usually helps me break free from stuck thinking is watching my favourite gaming channel. It really pulls me in, so my mind gets too busy to keep looping on the sticky thoughts."

Tip: think of things that get you moving, that make you feel good about yourself, that connect you with people, or that you enjoy.

When you get stuck in thinking loops late at night, what helps you settle down for sleep?

Ways to start
  • "When I can't switch my mind off at night, I try to…"
  • "If I'm caught in stuck thinking at night, I try to calm my mind down by…"

The important part: tell us why your idea works for you – like how it puts some space between you and your thoughts, or gives your busy mind something gentle to focus on, so it can settle enough to fall asleep.

Example"When I can't switch my mind off at night, I try to give it something else to focus on. There's this alphabet game where I pick a category, like food, and think of one for every letter – A: apple, B: banana, C: chocolate. It keeps my mind just busy enough to get off my worries, but it's relaxing, so I usually fall asleep before I get to Z."

No idea what to say? Nikki has sent you an online activity with 6 ideas for breaking free from night-time stuck thinking. Try them all, then pick one that you think would work well for you, and talk about that one.

How do you use problem-solving skills to help you break free from stuck thinking?

Ways to start
  • "If I'm stuck thinking about a problem I can actually do something about, I try to…"
  • "There was this one time I couldn't stop thinking about… what helped me was…"

Ideas you could talk about:

  • Brainstorming different ways to deal with the problem
  • Making a pros and cons list to help you decide
  • Asking a trusted friend or adult for help if you're not sure what's best
Example"There was this one time I couldn't stop worrying about a big school project. So I brainstormed a few ways to get started, then made a quick pros and cons list to pick the best one. Once I had a plan, my brain finally stopped worrying and I could move onto working on the project."

What are some everyday habits that help you have fewer sticky thoughts?

Ways to start
  • "Some habits that really help me are…"
  • "Having a good routine really helps keep my mind clear. For instance…"

Ideas you could talk about:

  • Making time for fun activities that grab your full attention
  • Journalling to get thoughts out of your head (but not too close to bedtime)
  • Writing down one thing you like about yourself and one you're grateful for each evening
  • Starting the day with something you love or that keeps you calm (a favourite playlist, some stretching, slow breathing)
  • Staying organised with a planner, to-do list or reminders, so you're not holding it all in your head
  • Talking regularly with people who listen and help you think in more helpful ways
Example"One habit that really helps me is writing down one thing I'm grateful for every night. It puts something good in my head instead of the worries."
